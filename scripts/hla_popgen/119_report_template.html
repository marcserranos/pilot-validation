Novel HLA alleles: how many, how sure, how many submittable

0. Three terms used throughout

Call. For every person, every one of their two chromosome copies (haplotypes) and every HLA gene, the pipeline (Immuannot, run on the long-read assemblies) reports one allele name, e.g. B*07:02:01:01. That is one call: one observation in one person. 12,233 people × 2 haplotypes × ~14 genes gives hundreds of thousands of calls. A novel call is one the pipeline could not match to any allele in its own (old, release 3.55) reference list, so it names the closest known allele and marks the rest as “new”.

Rebuildable call. For a novel call the pipeline does not hand over the new sequence directly. It gives the closest known allele plus a list of differences from it. A call is rebuildable when we can take that known allele's public sequence, apply the list of differences, and get the complete novel coding sequence back, with every changed base known. 137 of the 8,015 novel calls cannot be rebuilt (the difference list stops short of the whole sequence, or disagrees with the reference), so we cannot say exactly what is different and leave them out. “Substitution-only” means the differences are all single-base swaps, with no inserted or deleted bases; those are the only calls we trust (section 2).

Signature. A signature is the fingerprint of one candidate allele: gene + closest known allele + the exact set of base swaps. If ten people carry the same new allele, that is ten calls (or more, if some carry it on both chromosomes) but one signature. So “675 calls collapse to 441 signatures” means: 675 person-haplotype observations turned out to be only 441 different candidate alleles; 392 of those were seen in a single person and 49 in two or more people. The new scripts (118/119) count by the resulting coding sequence rather than by closest-allele-plus-swaps, which merges the rare cases where two different closest alleles lead to the same new sequence; I call that unit an allele from section 1 on, and the numbers can differ slightly from the signature counts.

1. The short answer

What counts as one "novel allele" here, and what is excluded

Unit. One distinct observed coding sequence (CDS) per gene. Script 91 (used in the S08 numbers below) keys on reference allele + substitution set, so it counts signatures; 118 collapses signatures that reach the same CDS, so the two counts can differ slightly.

Pool = "minimally novel". (i) Immuannot labelled the call "new" against its own (old, 3.55) reference set; (ii) the CDS can be rebuilt from the public reference allele plus the alignment string ("rebuildable"), so we know exactly which bases differ; (iii) it carries no insertion or deletion; (iv) it is not an exact CDS of a named allele in the newest IPD-IMGT/HLA release (3.66.0, 14 Oct 2026, verified on the GitHub mirror). A short-read check is then a confidence axis, not a gate: you decide below how much of it to require.

What is not in the pool. Indel-bearing calls (≈94% of "novel-protein" calls; most are consensus errors, see section 2); calls whose cs string cannot be replayed; novelty beyond the CDS (introns/UTRs; CDS identical); KIR and other non-HLA genes.

Why indels are dropped: the evidence

Of 7,635 novel-protein calls, 86% are indel-only and 93% carry a frameshift; at 1 edit from the reference, 91% are indel-only and 72% of those sit in homopolymer or tandem runs. Indels do not recur at fixed loci (462 distinct sites for 486 events), and 41 of 140 indel-carrying cases restore the frame with a second indel. Indel-bearing novel calls per person are ≈2× higher on Sequel II than on Revio at every ancestry, whereas substitution-only calls are platform-flat. Short reads agree: only 4% of indels are supported (79% not seen, 8% contradicted). Reading: the indel “novelty” is assembly-consensus error, not new alleles.

3. How the short reads validate a call

The novel sequence comes from a long-read assembly, so the short reads (srWGS, a different instrument and chemistry) are the independent witness. For every carrier and every substitution that makes the allele novel we ask: do this person's own short reads carry the base that the assembly says is there?

Turning counts into a confidence number

With k reads of n carrying the called base, the log10 likelihood ratio (LOD) of “the variant sits on this haplotype” versus “it is absent and the reads are just noise” is LOD = k·log10(p1/e) + (n−k)·log10((1−p1)/(1−e)), with p1 = 0.5 at a heterozygous site (the other haplotype has a different base) or 0.98 at a homozygous one, and e = 0.02 as background (sequencing error, mis-assigned reads, residual reference bias). LOD ≥ 3 means 1,000:1. A carrier's score is its weakest site; an allele's level comes from its best carrier, and “independent support” counts carriers with score ≥ 3 after removing relatives.

What the short reads said in the sampled S08 cases (static, 567 cases / 1,097 sites)

Indels: 79% not seen, 8% contradicted, 4% supported. Protein-altering substitutions: 60% supported, 13% “half the reads” (other haplotype call may differ), 18% not seen. Synonymous: 68% supported. Of 439 substitution-only cases with short reads, 281 had every checked site supported (189 with a protein-altering change). These come from a stratified sample, not population rates.

7. Saturation: how fast do we run out of new alleles?

Random orderings of persons; at every size the number of distinct alleles seen so far (median line, 5th-95th percentile band, 100 orderings). Only genes we call, only the pool, and the subsets we are fairly sure are real. A curve that flattens means the cohort has found most of what this method can find; a curve still rising means more people give more alleles.

What our data can and cannot satisfy

  • Satisfied by construction: phased haplotype (long-read assembly), indel-free CDS, nearest allele known, germline source.
  • Satisfied only if the bucket says so: a second independent method (short reads; LOD ≥ 3), replication in more than one person, transmission.
  • Not checkable from the VM table: full-length genomic extent beyond the CDS, two-field typing of the donor for A/B/C/DRB1, how IPD weighs short-read confirmation versus Sanger. These need a human look at the guideline.

9. Should we also validate with long reads? My take

Yes, but for a different question. The assembly itself is made from long reads, so a long-read pileup is not independent of the call; it cannot rescue a systematic error. What it adds: (1) it catches consensus errors — the 86% indel problem — because a pileup at the exact position shows whether individual reads agree or the assembler invented the base; (2) it tests phase, which IPD requires and short reads cannot give; (3) it gives per-read evidence to attach to a submission. Script 105 already does this for recurrent signatures against GRCh38 BAMs; its weakness is the same reference bias as any HLA pileup, so use it as a negative filter (reject) rather than a positive one. The strongest independent evidence we have is not a read pileup at all: transmission across platforms (child long-read, parent short-read) and RNA (the two HLA-F alleles are transcribed). I would add long-read pileup as one more column once the short-read table exists, not as a replacement.

10. Limits and disclosure

  • Counts of 1-19 participants are never written outside the Workbench: the public build shows classes 1 / 2-19 and exact values only from 20 up. The exact-count build must be created and kept on the VM.
  • Short-read evidence exists only for carriers that were extracted (90 extract --exhaustive-snv cap) and not for DRB3/4/5; “not evaluated” is a bucket, not a failure.
  • Ancestry labels are AoU predictions; relatedness is thinned with a greedy rule, so unrelated counts are lower bounds.
  • e = 0.02 and the ≤15-edit cut-off are judgement calls (S08 log, 10-23); the selector lets you move the bars, but not e. Re-run 118 with another e to change the LOD itself.
