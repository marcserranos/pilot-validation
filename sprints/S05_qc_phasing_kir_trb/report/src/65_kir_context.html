This part steps back: why KIR matters, what the field already knows, and where 12,000 long-read KIR haplotypes would add something new. Sources are cited inline. Claims marked (to verify) come from the literature but were not checked against the original paper for this report.

What KIR genes do

Natural killer (NK) cells kill virus-infected and tumour cells without prior training. They decide whom to kill by reading the HLA class I molecules on other cells. Healthy cells show HLA class I; many infected or cancerous cells lose it. KIR genes encode NK receptors for exactly this reading:

  • Inhibitory KIR (names with "L", long tail, such as KIR2DL1 and KIR3DL1) send a "stop" signal when they bind self-HLA. A cell that lost its HLA no longer sends that signal, so it gets killed ("missing self").
  • Activating KIR (names with "S", short tail, such as KIR2DS1 and KIR3DS1) send a "go" signal. Their ligands are less well known.
  • The number in the name is the count of extracellular domains (2D or 3D).

Each KIR binds a specific group of HLA molecules. That is what links Part 8 (KIR) with the HLA work in Parts 2–6:

Education. An NK cell only becomes fully responsive if its inhibitory KIR has found a matching HLA ligand in its own body. A person with KIR3DL1 but no Bw4 HLA has KIR3DL1+ NK cells that stay weak. So a KIR gene matters only together with the person's HLA. Function is a property of the KIR–HLA pair, and the strength depends on the specific alleles (Pugh et al., Life Sci Alliance 2019, to verify; link). Because KIR is on chr19 and HLA on chr6, the two are inherited independently (separate chromosomes), so KIR–HLA combinations are reshuffled every generation.

A and B haplotypes

Almost every KIR haplotype carries the four framework genes (3DL3, 3DP1, 2DL4, 3DL2); rare contracted haplotypes lack some of them. Between them it carries a variable set of others. Group A haplotypes have a fixed gene content, mostly inhibitory, with KIR2DS4 as the only activating gene (often a non-functional allele). Group B haplotypes vary in content and carry one or more activating genes. A recombination hotspot between 3DP1 and 2DL4 splits each haplotype into a centromeric and a telomeric half, each "A-like" or "B-like" (Roe et al., Genes Immun 2017, link). Part 8's "B-content share" counts haplotypes with B-like gene content anywhere: it runs from 32% (EAS) to 57% (SAS) in our unrelated cohort.

Why it matters: known associations

  • Pregnancy. Mothers with two A haplotypes (no activating KIR2DS1) carrying a fetus with HLA-C2 from the father have higher risk of pre-eclampsia, fetal growth restriction and recurrent miscarriage. Maternal KIR2DS1 protects (Hiby et al., J Clin Invest 2010, link; first shown by Hiby et al., J Exp Med 2004, to verify).
  • HIV. KIR3DS1 together with HLA-B Bw4-80Ile is associated with slower progression to AIDS (Martin et al., Nat Genet 2002, to verify). Specific KIR3DL1 alleles combined with HLA-B*57 give some of the strongest known host effects on HIV control (Martin et al., Nat Genet 2007, to verify).
  • Hepatitis C. People homozygous for KIR2DL3 and for HLA-C1 clear the virus more often (Khakoo et al., Science 2004).
  • Stem-cell transplantation. For acute myeloid leukaemia, unrelated donors with B haplotypes, especially centromeric B content, give lower relapse and better survival (Cooley et al., Blood 2010, to verify). Some transplant centres use a "B-content score" when choosing donors. A paediatric study of haploidentical transplants for acute lymphoblastic leukaemia found a similar benefit of donor B content (Oevermann et al., Blood 2014).
  • Autoimmunity and cancer. Associations with psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis (KIR2DS1/2DS2), type 1 diabetes and ankylosing spondylitis have been reported, mostly in modest-sized studies with mixed replication (to verify individually).

The state of the art in typing KIR

KIR is among the hardest regions of the genome to genotype, for the reasons Part 8 shows: copy number varies, genes are 90–98% identical, and fusions and duplications occur. The field has three approaches.

  1. Imputation from SNP arrays (KIR*IMP; Vukcevic et al., Am J Hum Genet 2015, to verify). It predicts gene presence or absence from nearby SNPs and is accurate mainly in European-ancestry data.
  2. Short-read callers for WGS, WES or RNA-seq: PING, T1K (Genome Res 2023), Geny (Front Immunol 2024), kir-mapper. These estimate copy number and alleles from k-mers or alignments. The largest application is KIRCLE, which called KIR alleles from exomes of ~60,000 TCGA and UK Biobank samples and found only exploratory associations (BMC Biol 2022, to verify). Benchmarks use a few dozen reference genomes, and accuracy in non-European ancestries is poorly known.
  3. Long-read assemblies: full haplotypes with gene order. The human pangenome (HPRC) work annotated 94 KIR haplotypes from 47 people, found 570 new alleles and structural variants in ~16% of haplotypes (Genome Res 2024, link). Immuannot, the annotator we use, was published in the same year (Genome Res 2024).

KIR has been imputed into biobank SNP data for association work, but we found no KIR × HLA interaction scan at biobank scale built on long-read KIR haplotypes. The largest long-read KIR collections so far have tens to hundreds of haplotypes. This cohort has 17,905 complete KIR haplotypes from 11,882 unrelated people with KIR calls in six ancestries (Part 8; the HLA-called unrelated set is 11,856).

What we could do with it

1 · A long-read KIR reference panel

Use the ~18,000 complete haplotypes as truth to measure, by ancestry, how well short-read KIR callers and imputation perform on the same people (All of Us also has short-read genomes for them). Then use the panel to impute KIR into the hundreds of thousands of short-read participants. This is the KIR version of our idea #1 (short-read error by ancestry), and probably the highest-value use. Needs: run one short-read caller on the overlap; the panel exists.

2 · KIR–HLA functional pairs by ancestry

Per person, count which inhibitory KIR have their HLA ligand (2DL1+C2, 2DL2/3+C1, 3DL1+Bw4, 3DL2+A3/A11) using our phased HLA calls and complete KIR haplotypes. Describe how "educated NK repertoires" differ by ancestry, and test whether KIR and HLA ligand frequencies co-vary across populations more than chance (co-evolution). Script 48 already computes ligand co-occurrence and is a starting point.

3 · Structural variants and new alleles

Catalogue contracted and expanded haplotypes, fusion genes and novel alleles with the recurrence and read-support filters used for HLA in script 64. The pangenome found SVs in 16% of 94 haplotypes; at 18,000 haplotypes even rare structures show up many times, by ancestry.

4 · Disease associations, done right

With idea 1 or directly on the long-read cohort: replicate the classic pairs (HIV, pregnancy outcomes, transplant-relevant B content, autoimmunity) in All of Us health records, then a KIR × HLA-ligand PheWAS. The long-read cohort alone (12k) is small for disease work, so this depends on imputation (idea 1).

KIR expression in blood RNA-seq is low at gene level (Part 7). NK cells are ~5–15% of blood lymphocytes, and each NK cell expresses only some of its KIR genes, so expression studies of KIR would need allele-level counting and cell-type adjustment. That makes it a weaker direction than the four above. KIR also appears on some CD8 T cells, but we found no published work linking KIR genotype to the TCR repertoire, so a KIR × TRB analysis would be exploratory.
