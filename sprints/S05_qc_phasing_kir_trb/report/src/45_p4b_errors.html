Part 4bWhere the errors happen, and whether pruning people helps

Question. Part 3 measured how often calls are wrong; Part 4 showed that window depth does not give a useful filter. This part asks the sharper version: which calls and which people carry the typing and phasing errors, whether they cluster at low coverage, and whether removing the worst x% of people makes the rest of the cohort cleaner.

Questioning the premise first

"Prune the worst-covered x% of people" sounds natural, but four things about it needed checking before running it.

  1. Depth where? A person's depth over the whole 4 Mb MHC window says little about one gene. A gene can be thin in a person whose window is normal, because of local structure. So the experiments below use depth at the gene of each call, normalised to the cohort median for that gene and platform (1.0 = typical), as well as the window.
  2. Part of it was already done. Script 59 (Part 4) found that no window-depth cut removes a useful share of bad outcomes, and that low KIR depth is partly caused by contracted haplotypes. Removing thin people would remove real biology.
  3. Errors belong to calls, not people. A person with one thin gene has seven good ones. Dropping the person loses all eight. Flagging the one call keeps the seven.
  4. Who gets removed matters. If thin coverage or fragmented assemblies are commoner in some ancestries, pruning shifts the cohort's make-up. That can bias every frequency estimate more than the errors it removes.

So the experiment (script 67) measures both: where errors sit along depth at the gene, and what pruning by each candidate metric does, compared with removing the same number of people at random.

From which file, by what process (67)
Data in
  • Per-person, per-gene depth from 58 (~/s05/internal/58/per_person_depth.tsv, VM only), MAPQ ≥ 0.
  • Table 1 calls with the contig each call sits on (Part 2).
  • Relative pairs: the first-degree pairs that share an MHC haplotype (the 56b filter of Part 3).
  • Trios: the 179 yak-validated trios with each child gene's parent of origin (57b).
  • Switch errors: per-trio switch and Hamming rates from yak trioeval (57, the 50 trios run at full depth), and the per-gene parent of origin along each child's contigs (57b).
  • Per-person outcomes from 59 (KIR incomplete, MHC fragmented, HLA gene absent) for the 11,856 unrelated people.
Process
  1. Pairs (A). One record per pair and gene: "error" when the two relatives share no 2-field allele. Each record gets the lower and the higher of the two relatives' depths at that gene. Records are binned into a 3 × 3 grid (thirds of depth) and into tenths of each axis, with Wilson 95% intervals. A logistic regression gives the odds ratio for error per 0.1 lower depth, adjusted for the call confidence score.
  2. Trios (B). One record per child gene with a known parent of origin: "error" when the child's allele is not among that parent's two alleles. Same binning on child depth and transmitting-parent depth.
  3. Switches (C). Spearman correlation, across trio children, of the switch-error rate with (a) MHC depth and (b) the number of contigs the MHC is split into. Then, walking along each child haplotype, every step between two neighbouring genes on the same contig is a "switch" if the parent of origin changes. Steps are classed by MHC zone (class I, class III, DR–DQ, DP, at hg38 boundaries 31.4, 32.4 and 32.95 Mb).
  4. Pruning (D). Rank the unrelated people by a metric (MHC window depth, KIR window depth, MHC contigs per haplotype), remove the worst 0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 15, 20 or 30%, and recompute each outcome among those kept. Repeat with the same number removed at random (20 draws) as a null. Record the ancestry and platform make-up of the removed, and re-score pair and trio errors with the same cut applied to every person.
Trade-offs
  • A plot with one dot per pair, coloured by agreement, would be the most direct picture, but each dot is one person's depth and genotype agreement, which is participant-level data under the All of Us rules. The binned grid shows the same pattern with every cell over 20.
  • Gene depth is measured on reads aligned to GRCh38, while the calls come from the assemblies, so depth is a proxy for how much data the assembler had.
  • Pair and trio errors involve two people, so "low depth" is defined by the lower one; the higher one is kept as a second axis.
Unit
A pair × gene (A), a child gene (B), a trio child or a step between neighbouring genes (C), a person (D).

Phasing switches follow fragmentation, not depth, and sit in the long gaps between genes

A switch error is a phasing mistake: along one assembled haplotype, the sequence jumps from the mother's chromosome to the father's. Yak trioeval (Part 2) counts these with k-mers that are unique to each parent. Script 67 asked two things: which children have more switches, and where along the MHC the switches happen.

No association of switch rate with depth was detected (with 50 children the power is limited: the MHC correlation of −0.17 has a 95% interval of about −0.4 to +0.1). Fragmentation does go with switches, in class I and on the chr20 control alike, so it is a property of how a given assembly was built, genome-wide, not something special about the MHC. The same fragmented assemblies have lower Hamming error on chr20 (correlation −0.88), which fits assemblies that break often and also mis-phase more often between breaks. DR–DQ, the region Cole's fine-mapping needs, shows neither association.

For Cole's DQA1~DQB1 and DR~DQ units this is good news: both genes sit inside the closely spaced DR–DQ block, where switches are rare, so a DRB1~DQ call can keep its phasing flag as is. Claims that join genes across the long gaps (class I to class II, or DQ to DP) should require the genes to be on one contig and still treat the join as uncertain.

Does pruning the worst people help?

Who gets removed. The bulk of a 30% prune mirrors the cohort (unrelated people are 25% AFR, 22% AMR, 12% EAS, 25% EUR, 4% MID, 10% SAS). The extreme tail does not. The worst 1–2% by MHC depth are 40–48% AMR (twice their share) and 39–50% Sequel IIe (about four to five times its share), and the worst 1–2% by contig count are 35–38% AMR and about half Sequel IIe. A small prune therefore removes mostly one ancestry and one platform. Where depth tracks genotype, as with contracted KIR haplotypes, that biases those groups' frequencies.

Answer to "should we prune the worst x%?"

No, flag calls instead. The evidence, in order:

  1. Typing errors are concentrated in calls with low depth at the gene: the thinnest fifth of relative-pair records holds about half of the disagreements, and the thinnest third of trio calls about half of the Mendel errors. Window depth, which a person-level prune would use, captures this poorly.
  2. Pruning people by depth removes up to 30% of the cohort for a 0.3–0.5 point gain in pair and trio error, and removes real contracted KIR haplotypes along the way.
  3. Pruning by fragmentation looks powerful only because it removes the outcome it is measured against.
  4. The worst tail is enriched for AMR and Sequel IIe, so small prunes bias ancestry-specific estimates.
  5. No link between phasing switches and depth was detected. Switches follow how fragmented the assembly is and concentrate across the long gaps between typed genes (MICA → DRB, TAP → DPA1), not within the DR–DQ block.

Proposed rule: add a per-call flag "gene depth below 0.75 of the platform median" (the cut that set aside about the thinnest 20% of pair records here; the share of single calls it flags, and its effect at exactly 0.75, still need computing), report main results with and without flagged calls, and subtract one point from the confidence score for flagged calls. For phasing, mark any haplotype claim that joins genes across the MICA → DRB or TAP → DPA1 gaps as lower confidence, whatever the contig says. This needs Marc's and Cole's agreement before it changes any published number (Decisions).
