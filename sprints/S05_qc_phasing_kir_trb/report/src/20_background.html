Before readingHow to read this report

The report has three layers. Read the summary first. Then use the background course as a reference: open a module whenever a result relies on an idea you have not met. Every result section uses the same three blocks.

Data trail (blue edge)

This block traces each result from start to finish:

  • which file it started from, and what one row of that file is;
  • what was done to it, step by step;
  • which trade-offs were made and why;
  • which unit was counted (people, haplotypes, calls, allele sequences) and over which denominator.

That last point is the "axis" a big dataset gets collapsed onto. If you know it, you know what a percentage means.

Figure caption

Shows describes what is drawn. How to read says where to look. Meaning gives the interpretation and the numbers. Limits says what the figure cannot tell you.

Significance (dashed box)

How big the effect is and how sure we are, measured against something you can picture. It also says what changes for the paper because of it.

Review (red edge)

An independent review checked the methods, the numbers and the wording. Where it found a problem, the red block says what was wrong and what changed.

Every number comes from a committed table in the public repo; the file is named under each figure. Counts of 1–19 people are never shown (see masking). Allele names and sequence counts are not people and are shown as is.

The data each person has, and how an HLA call is made

About 13,000 All of Us participants were sequenced with PacBio HiFi long reads. These are reads of ~15–20 kb that are about 99.9% accurate. For each person All of Us provides several files, and the project's earlier pipeline built one table on top of them.

What a row of Table 1 says. On haplotype 1 of person X, on contig h1tg000012l at positions S–E, there is HLA-DQB1 copy 1, and its sequence best matches DQB1*06:02:01:01. When nothing in the database matches exactly, the row records how it differs (novelty_depth, explained in MHC and HLA alleles). It records the exact coding change (cds_mut), how far the sequence is from the closest known allele (template_distance), whether the caller was unsure (template_warning), and whether several known alleles tied (n_tied).

Where the work runs. Participant-level files never leave the All of Us Workbench, a cloud VM. Scripts run there and write two kinds of output. INTERNAL tables (one row per person) stay on the VM. PUBLIC tables are aggregates, with small counts masked; these are copied into this public repo, and every figure is drawn from them.

Three sequencing platforms. Revio (newest, 11,015 people with calls) and Sequel IIe (1,218) went through the production pipeline. The 991 Sequel II people have assemblies but no HLA or KIR calls; Part 10 explains why. The platform also stands for the sequencing site, the period and the assembler version, so a "platform effect" mixes all of these.

Assembly, haplotypes, contigs and phasing

You carry two copies of chromosome 6, one from each parent. A haplotype is the sequence of one of those copies. The question "which alleles sit together on the same copy?" is called phasing. It matters because HLA genes act, and are inherited, as combinations along one chromosome.

The assembler (hifiasm) overlaps reads into long continuous pieces called contigs. Where the two parental copies differ, it separates them, so it produces two sets of contigs, labelled hap1 and hap2. Where the copies are identical over a long stretch, the reads cannot tell the copies apart and the contig usually ends. The next contig is then labelled hap1 or hap2 arbitrarily: hap1 of the next piece may be the other parent's copy.

So two kinds of "same haplotype" exist. If two genes sit on one contig, they are physically phased: one stretch of DNA joins them. If they sit on different contigs that merely share the label "hap1", nothing ties them together. In the picture, hap1 pairs A and B correctly but joins them to the mother's C. That is why the report keeps asking "same contig?", and why Part 2 measures how often one contig really holds one parent's DNA.

Two error types are measured later. A switch error is a short flip from one parent's sequence to the other inside a contig. A hamming error is the share of a contig that belongs to the "wrong" parent overall, which captures long-range mixing.

The MHC, HLA genes, allele names and "novel" alleles

The MHC is a ~4 Mb region of chromosome 6 that holds the HLA genes. Their proteins show fragments of proteins (peptides) to T cells. Class I (HLA-A, -B, -C) is on nearly all cells and presents to CD8 T cells. Class II (DR, DQ, DP) is on immune cells and presents to CD4 T cells. They are the most variable genes in the human genome, and the strongest genetic risk factors for most autoimmune diseases.

Reading an allele name

"Novel" alleles and novelty depth. When the sequence matches no named allele, Immuannot reports the closest one and the level at which they differ. Novelty depth 2 means a new protein (the amino acids differ). Depth 3 means the same protein with a silent DNA change. Depth 4 means a difference outside the coding sequence only. A key is one distinct novel sequence: gene plus exact coding change. Five people carrying the same new sequence are one key with five carriers. Keys count alleles, not people.

DQ pairing groups (G1/G2). The DQ molecule is built from two chains, α (from DQA1) and β (from DQB1). Biochemistry says only some α/β combinations fold into a working molecule. Group 1 (G1) is DQA1*02–06 with DQB1*02–04. Group 2 (G2) is DQA1*01 with DQB1*05–06. Cross-group pairs are thought not to work, and a haplotype that encodes one is expected to be rare or absent. Part 6 tests that.

DR paralogs. Next to DRB1, a chromosome carries none or one of DRB3, DRB4 or DRB5, depending on the DRB1 lineage. These are the DR51 (DRB5), DR52 (DRB3) and DR53 (DRB4) haplotype groups. It is a known rule, so a method that finds it on its own is working (Part 6).

KIR genes and KIR haplotypes

KIR genes (on chr19) encode receptors on natural killer cells that read HLA class I molecules. Unlike HLA, KIR haplotypes differ in which genes they carry at all, not only in alleles: a chromosome carries roughly 7 to 15 KIR genes. Four framework genes are present on nearly every haplotype and mark its structure: KIR3DL3 at the start (centromeric end), 3DP1 and 2DL4 in the middle, and 3DL2 at the end (telomeric end).

Why KIR is hard to assemble. KIR genes are near-copies of each other (~90–98% identical), so contigs often stop inside the cluster. A complete haplotype in this report has all its KIR genes on one contig, with both end genes (3DL3 and 3DL2) present. Only on complete haplotypes can we say a gene is truly absent; elsewhere, "missing" may mean the contig ended early.

Allelic pairs. 2DL2 and 2DL3 are two forms of the same position, as are 3DL1 and 3DS1. A haplotype normally carries one or the other. Seeing both on one haplotype is either a rare real duplication or an assembly artefact, and Part 8 tells them apart.

The TRB locus, V(D)J recombination and the T-cell repertoire

Each T cell carries a T-cell receptor built from a β chain and an α chain. The β chain gene TRB (chr7, ~0.6 Mb) is stored in pieces: about 65 V segments (TRBV…), 2 D, 13 J and 2 C. As each T cell matures, it cuts its own DNA and joins one V to one D and one J. This is V(D)J recombination, and it permanently deletes the DNA between the joined pieces in that cell. A person's repertoire is the mix of receptors across their T cells, and RNA sequencing can measure how often each V segment is used.

Two facts follow, and both appear in the results. First, a gene that is missing from the germline cannot be used. About 27% of people never use TRBV4-3 (Aleix's repertoire work), and TRBV4-3 sits on a known optional insertion; Part 9 measures who carries it. Second, blood DNA is partly T-cell DNA. Because recombination deletes the segments between the joined V and J, the read depth over the D–J region and the nearby V genes is somatically reduced. That is not a germline genotype (Part 4).

Reference genome detail. The standard reference, GRCh38, describes the chromosome without the insertion. The insertion exists only on an "alternative" contig (ALT, named KI270803v1). The HiFi reads were aligned to GRCh38 without ALTs, so reads from TRBV4-3 have no matching place in the reference, and that complicates genotyping.

Reads, depth, mapping quality, alignment identity and allele fraction

Aligning reads to the reference stacks them over the positions they came from. The number of reads covering a base is the depth. Here it is about 20 on average.

  • Relative depth divides a region's depth by the same person's depth over neutral control windows. That cancels how much DNA that person was sequenced with. 1.0 means "covered like the rest of the genome". A one-copy deletion should give 0.5 and a duplication 1.5.
  • Mapping quality (MAPQ) is the aligner's confidence that a read is placed correctly. It is 0 when a read fits equally well in two places (paralogs) and up to 60 when the placement is unique. Counting depth at MAPQ ≥ 0 keeps ambiguous reads; MAPQ ≥ 20 drops them.
  • Alignment identity is the share of matching bases in an alignment block. Contig-to-reference alignments come in a table format called PAF, one line per block, which lists the matching bases and the block length. True copies of a region align at ~95–99.9%. Repeats that merely resemble it align much lower (Part 10).
  • Allele fraction: at a site where two versions exist, it is the share of reads that carry one version. A heterozygote should show ~0.5 and a homozygote 0 or 1. Each read is a draw, so with 14 reads a true 0.5 still scatters roughly between 0.3 and 0.7.

Homopolymers: the main HiFi error

A homopolymer is a run of one repeated base, like AAAAAAA. HiFi reads and assemblers sometimes miscount its length by one. A 1-base insertion or deletion inside a gene's coding sequence shifts the reading frame and produces a "new protein" that does not exist. Part 5 shows this artefact explains most recurrent "novel" classical HLA proteins.

Relatives, kinship, the unrelated set, trios and k-mers

Kinship coefficient. This is the probability that an allele picked at random from person 1 and one from person 2 at the same position are copies of the same ancestral allele. It is 0.5 for identical twins or duplicate samples, 0.25 for parent–child and full siblings, 0.125 for second degree and 0.0625 for third degree. All of Us provides it for every pair above ~0.044.

Unrelated set. Frequencies must not count one family several times. Pairs with kinship ≥ 0.0442 (the usual third-degree cut, 2−4.5) are broken by repeatedly removing the person with the most relatives. Among HLA-called people this leaves 11,856.

Trios. When a sequenced person has two first-degree relatives who are not related to each other, those two must be the parents. This finds 179 parent–parent–child trios.

k-mers and parent of origin (yak). A k-mer is a short word of DNA, here 31 letters. The tool yak lists all k-mers in each parent's reads. A k-mer in the child's contig that occurs only in the father's reads marks that stretch as paternal; one only in the mother's marks it maternal. Colouring a child's contig this way shows directly whether it is one parent's DNA, and so whether phasing is right.

Mendelian check. A child's allele must be present in the parent who transmitted it. If the transmitting parent (known from the k-mers) does not carry the child's called allele, then the child's call or the parent's call is wrong.

Population genetics: frequencies, Hardy–Weinberg, LD and ancestry labels

Allele frequency is the share of chromosomes (haplotypes) carrying an allele. With two chromosomes per person, 11,856 unrelated people give 23,712 haplotypes.

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE). If an insertion has frequency p and people pair their chromosomes at random, the expected genotype shares are p² (two copies), 2p(1−p) (one copy) and (1−p)² (none). Real populations follow this closely for most variants. A big shortage of heterozygotes usually means the genotyping is wrong, or that a group mixes sub-populations with different frequencies (the Wahlund effect).

Linkage disequilibrium (LD). Alleles of nearby genes are inherited together across generations, so some combinations are far more common, and others far rarer, than chance predicts. In the MHC, LD spans megabases. The expected count under independence is E = n × pA × pB: how often a combination would appear if the two genes were shuffled. Comparing it with the observed count shows LD. An observed 0 where E is 1,500 is a striking absence.

Predicted ancestry. All of Us assigns each person to one of six continental groups (AFR, AMR, EAS, EUR, MID, SAS) from genome-wide genetic similarity to reference panels. Each person also gets a probability for each group. "Purity p" is the highest of these. Admixed people, common in AMR, MID and EUR as labelled here, have lower p. These are genetic-similarity labels, not self-identity.

Small-count masking and complementary suppression

All of Us forbids publishing any count of 1–19 participants, because small groups can identify people. Such counts are written <20, and rates computed from them are blanked. Masking one cell is not enough when a table also shows its total and the other parts: the masked cell is a subtraction away. Complementary suppression then masks a second cell, written <20*, so the subtraction no longer works.

This sprint made the rule generic (_disclosure.py), applied it to every new public table and tested it. A few older tables still have the old problem; they are listed in Part 11.
